"There is no such thing as a one-page CV rule" and "your CV must be one page" are both said with total confidence. Neither is quite right. Length is a signal, and the right signal depends on who is reading.

## The short version

- **Early career or career change:** one page.
- **Most mid-level roles:** one to two pages.
- **Senior, specialist or academic:** two pages, and sometimes more.

That is the whole rule. Everything below is the reasoning, so you can apply it to your own situation.

## Why one page works when it does

A single page forces prioritisation. When you cannot fit everything, you have to choose the most relevant achievements — which is exactly what a recruiter wants. It also signals clarity: this person knows what matters.

The catch is that one page only works if you have roughly one page worth of relevant material. Squeezing fifteen years into a page by shrinking the font to 8pt achieves the opposite of clarity.

## Why two pages is fine

For anyone with several roles, two pages give you room to show progression, quantify results and include a proper skills section. Recruiters hiring for experienced roles expect two pages. They will not penalise a well-organised second page.

What they will penalise is a second page that is 60% empty, or one that pads out a thin early career with hobbies and references.

## The relevance test

Before trimming, rank your content by one question: **does this help me get this specific job?**

Sort everything into three buckets:

1. **Essential** — proves you can do the job.
2. **Supporting** — shows depth, context or trajectory.
3. **Nice to have** — true and mildly interesting.

If you are over your target length, cut from bucket three first, then the weakest items in bucket two. Keep bucket one intact even if it means going slightly over.

## What to cut before you cut substance

In rough order:

- References and referee contact details — use "available on request".
- Every hobby except the ones that connect to the role.
- School results once you have a degree and a few years of experience.
- Software you used once, four jobs ago.
- Repeating the same point in the summary and the experience section.

## What never to cut

- Numbers and results.
- Anything explicitly listed in the job advert.
- Your contact details and a link to a portfolio if you have one.

## A note on length and ATS

Applicant tracking systems parse text, so a longer CV is not automatically worse for them. What hurts is complex layout. If you are applying somewhere that uses older software, a single-column template with clear headings is the safer choice — and CV Maker labels which templates are the most conservative.

## The final test

After you have decided, read the CV as if you were hiring for the role. If you find yourself skimming or bored, the problem is not the page count — it is the content. Trim the weakest bullet, not the strongest page.
